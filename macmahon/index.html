MacMahon's squares · 1921

Twenty-four squares,
three colours,
1.75 × 10³⁸ arrangements.

Each piece is a square cut into four triangles. The task is to lay all of them into a 6 × 4 rectangle so that touching edges agree and the whole border is one colour. It sounds harmless. The arithmetic disagrees.

01 — The puzzle

Four rules, twenty-four tiles

  1. 01All twenty-four pieces must be used.
  2. 02Each piece may be rotated in four directions.
  3. 03Edges that touch must share the same colour.
  4. 04The twenty outer edges are all red.

The puzzle was published by Percy MacMahon in New Mathematical Pastimes in 1921. The set is not an arbitrary selection: it contains every possible square that can be made from three colours.

That completeness is what makes it a good mathematical toy — and what makes it a terrible candidate for brute force, as the next two sections show.

02 — The pieces

From 81 colourings to 24 tiles

A square has four edges, each painted with one of three colours, so there are 34 = 81 colourings. But a tile you can rotate is the same tile: identifying colourings up to rotation leaves only 24 classes.

(3⁴ + 3¹ + 3² + 3¹) / 4 = 96 / 4 = 24

That is Burnside's lemma: average the colourings fixed by each of the four rotations. Three tiles are single-coloured, six use two colours, the rest use three. Here is the complete set.

R red · B white · L blue — the four letters are the north, east, south and west edges.

For scale: the observable universe contains something like 1080 atoms, so 1038 is not “astronomical” — it is merely hopeless. Checking a billion arrangements per second for fourteen billion years would still leave essentially all of them untouched.

04 — What it actually takes

Nobody enumerates them

Real solvers fill the grid cell by cell and reject a piece the moment it fails to match its neighbours. The red border does most of the work: it pins down the twenty outer edges before the search starts.

Even so, solutions are scarce. There are 12 261 ways to complete the 6 × 4 board — a figure produced by a 1964 computer run that took about forty hours.

Modern code does it in seconds, but the shape of the problem does not change: the useful work is pruning, not enumerating.

05 — The counting machine

Three machines, one board

The same 6 × 4 board is filled by three different machines. The first checks everything; each of the next two removes one thing. In every card the small board is the machine at work, slowed down to one tile every 100 ms so that you can watch it.

Step 1 — prune at the first mismatch

A tile is now placed only if it matches the tile on its left and the tile above. One test, and the whole subtree below the rejected placement is never explored: the boards that would have started with it are never built. In the preview you can see it — tiles appear, and when a branch dies they come back off one by one.

There is no simple total for this machine: the search is a tree of partial boards, not a flat list, and its exact size is not a number we can write down here. What changes is the work — the 10³⁸ complete boards are never built.

Step 2 — keep pruning, pin the all-red tile

Same machine as step 1, with one extra rule. Take the tile that is red on all four edges, RRRR, and forbid it anywhere outside the top-left quarter of the board. The four quarters are interchangeable — flipping the board top to bottom or left to right walks from one to the next — so one quarter is enough, and the search space is divided by four.

Pinning the all-red tile in one quarter divides the brute force by four: 1.4 × 10²⁹ years instead of 5.5 × 10²⁹. On a 38-digit number that is a rounding error. The step that changes the nature of the problem is the one before it — refusing a tile as soon as it does not fit.

06 — Complexity

Is it an NP problem?

In NP, yes. A filled board is checked in a single pass: 38 interior edges plus 20 border edges. The certificate is 24 tiles long and verification is linear.

NP-complete as a family, yes. The general question — can a given set of edge-coloured tiles cover a given region? — is NP-complete (Demaine & Demaine, 2007), and even the 1 × n case is NP-hard (Bosboom et al., 2017). MacMahon's squares are one instance of that family.

The 24-tile puzzle itself, no. A fixed instance has a fixed answer; it is a constant, not an asymptotic question. What is NP-complete is the problem parameterised by the number of tiles and colours.
