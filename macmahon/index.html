MacMahon's squares · 1921

MacMahon Squares

Edge-matching puzzle · JavaScript

Twenty-four squares, three colours, and 1.75 × 10³⁸ ways to arrange them. Each piece is a square cut into four triangles: the task is to lay them all into a 6 × 4 rectangle so that touching edges agree and the whole border is one colour. It sounds harmless. The arithmetic disagrees.

01 — The puzzle

Four rules, twenty-four tiles

  1. 01All twenty-four pieces must be used.
  2. 02Each piece may be rotated in four directions.
  3. 03Edges that touch must share the same colour.
  4. 04The twenty outer edges are all red.

The puzzle was published by Percy MacMahon in New Mathematical Pastimes in 1921. The set is not an arbitrary selection: it contains every possible square that can be made from three colours.

That completeness is what makes it a good mathematical toy — and what makes it a terrible candidate for brute force, as the next two sections show.

02 — The pieces

From 81 colourings to 24 tiles

A square has four edges, each painted with one of three colours, so there are 34 = 81 colourings. But a tile you can rotate is the same tile: identifying colourings up to rotation leaves only 24 classes.

(3⁴ + 3¹ + 3² + 3¹) / 4 = 96 / 4 = 24

That is Burnside's lemma: average the colourings fixed by each of the four rotations. Three tiles are single-coloured, six use two colours, the rest use three. Here is the complete set.

R red · B white · L blue — the four letters are the north, east, south and west edges.

For scale: the observable universe contains something like 1080 atoms, so 1038 is not “astronomical” — it is merely hopeless. Checking a billion arrangements per second for fourteen billion years would still leave essentially all of them untouched.

04 — What it actually takes

Nobody enumerates them

Real solvers fill the grid cell by cell and reject a piece the moment it fails to match its neighbours. The red border does most of the work: it pins down the twenty outer edges before the search starts.

Even so, solutions are scarce — scarce enough that counting them all took a dedicated computer run. The exact number is at the end of this page.

Modern code does it in seconds, but the shape of the problem does not change: the useful work is pruning, not enumerating.

05 — The counting machine

Five machines, one board

The same 6 × 4 board is filled by five different machines. The first checks everything; each of the next four removes one thing. In every card the small board is the machine at work, slowed down to one tile every 100 ms so that you can watch it.

Step 1 — prune at the first mismatch

A tile is now placed only if it matches the tile on its left and the tile above. One test, and the whole subtree below the rejected placement is never explored: the boards that would have started with it are never built. In the preview you can see it — tiles appear, and when a branch dies they come back off one by one.

There is no simple total for this machine: the search is a tree of partial boards, not a flat list, and its exact size is not a number we can write down here. What changes is the work — the 10³⁸ complete boards are never built.

Step 2 — keep pruning, pin the all-red tile

Same machine as step 1, with one extra rule. Take the tile that is red on all four edges, RRRR, and forbid it anywhere outside the top-left quarter of the board. The four quarters are interchangeable — flipping the board top to bottom or left to right walks from one to the next — so one quarter is enough, and the search space is divided by four.

Step 3 — stop rotating identical tiles

Three tiles look the same after a quarter turn — RRRR, WWWW and BBBB — so they have one useful orientation, not four. Three more are unchanged after a half turn (RWRW, RBRB, WBWB) and have two. The other eighteen really do have four.

Trying the four rotations of a monochrome tile builds the same subtree four times. Dropping the duplicates turns 4²⁴ into 4¹⁸ × 2³ = 2³⁹: the flat count is divided by 512, and the preview stops re-exploring boards it has already seen.

Step 4 — fill the most constrained cell first

So far the machine fills the board like a page of text: left to right, top to bottom. But the cells are not equally hard. A corner has two edges fixed by the red border, a side cell one, an interior cell none — the number of tiles that can still go there varies a lot.

At every step, look at all the empty cells, count how many placements each one still allows, and fill the one with the fewest options. The preview now builds the frame first and dives into the middle afterwards, and dead ends show up sooner.

Four refinements: a quarter for the all-red tile, a factor 512 for the duplicate rotations, the most constrained cell first, and one bad edge to kill a whole branch. The first three are constant factors and a better order of exploration. The last is the only real algorithm — and it is still not enough.

And it is still hopeless

This is where the NP question stops being abstract. Checking a filled board is linear: a few dozen edge comparisons. But the number of boards to check grows exponentially with the number of tiles, and pruning improves the constant in front of that exponential — never the exponent. A machine a billion times faster buys a few more tiles, not an answer.

So the way out is not a better search of the same space. It is a smaller space, solved first, then refined. Here is the trick.

Step 5 — solve a smaller problem first

Forget two of the three colours. Repaint every white and every blue triangle in the same pale blue, so that the board only knows red and not red. The twenty-four tiles stop being twenty-four: they collapse onto six patterns.

The reduced puzzle is much easier — six patterns with fixed multiplicities (six copies of the no-red pattern, eight of the single-red one, and so on) instead of twenty-four distinct tiles. Copies of the same pattern are indistinguishable, so the machine tracks how many of each remain — not which piece is where — and it only tries the distinct rotations of each pattern: one for the two solid patterns, two for the opposite-red one, four for the rest. And as in step 2, the all-red pattern is confined to the top-left quarter: the four quarters are the same quarter seen four times. With that restriction it has twenty solutions, and it is small enough to solve right here. Each one is a skeleton: for every edge of the board, it says red, or not red. The machine below keeps every trick of the previous steps — and, like step 4, it always fills the most constrained cell first.

Step 6 — recolour the skeletons

Now put the real tiles back. A skeleton dictates which edges must be red, so at every cell only the tiles whose red edges line up can go there: a red edge can never receive a white or a blue triangle. The enormous search has become a filling exercise inside a fixed frame — twenty frames, a handful of tiles each.

Recolour all twenty frames and you get 26 656 boards — every complete board whose all-red tile is in the top-left quarter. Four quarters, four times as many: the answer is 106 624.

06 — The answer

How many solutions are there?

106 624

One hundred and six thousand, six hundred and twenty-four ways to lay the twenty-four tiles into the 6 × 4 board so that every touching edge agrees and the twenty outer edges are red.

The page counts them in two passes: the twenty skeletons of step 5 give 26 656 boards when the all-red tile is in the top-left quarter, and the four quarters are interchangeable — 4 × 26 656 = 106 624. Against the 1.75 × 10³⁸ arrangements, that is about one solution in 1.6 × 10³³.

07 — How

How 10³⁸ becomes seven seconds

Step 0 checks ten boards a second. At that rate the 1.75 × 10³⁸ arrangements would take 5.5 × 10²⁹ years — about 4 × 10¹⁹ times the age of the universe. Steps 5 and 6 together take about seven seconds in a browser, on a laptop. That is a factor of 2.5 × 10³⁶.

Part of it is ordinary engineering. Steps 1 to 4 refuse a tile at the first bad edge, pin the all-red tile to one quarter, stop rotating identical tiles, and always fill the most constrained cell first. All of that is real — and all of it explores the same space, only faster.

The rest is not engineering. Step 5 changes the question: it stops looking at the 1.75 × 10³⁸ arrangements of twenty-four distinct tiles, projects the board onto red / not-red, and solves that quotient — twenty skeletons, six seconds. Step 6 then lifts each skeleton back to real tiles. Because a skeleton fixes which edges are red, the enormous search collapses into a small filling problem: 26 656 boards, half a second.

The lesson is not that the machine got fast. It is that almost none of the 10³⁸ carries useful information. A solution is constrained almost entirely by its red skeleton; once that skeleton is fixed, only a handful of choices remain. Ask the small question first, and the big one answers itself.
